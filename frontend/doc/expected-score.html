How the expected score is calculated

A study's expected score is the score you would get, on average, if you always played your prepared moves and your opponents chose their moves like the players in the Opening Explorer. This page describes the calculation exactly, with a worked example. The numbers in the example are made up, so that the arithmetic is easy to follow.

The idea

Your study is a tree of moves. On your side, it has one prepared move per position; on your opponent's side, it can have several moves, the replies you've prepared for. The calculation walks this tree from the study's starting point, with three rules:

  1. You always play your prepared move.
  2. Your opponent replies as players do in the Explorer. In each position, each move the Explorer lists gets a weight: its number of games, divided by the total of the listed moves.
  3. Where your preparation ends, the line counts with the results of its last move, as the Explorer lists them in the position before that move: its share of wins, draws and losses, scored as a win 1, a draw ½ and a loss 0, from your side.

The expected score is then the weighted average of the values where the lines end: the probability of reaching the end of each line, times its value, summed over all lines.

A worked example

A study for White in the Caro-Kann Fantasy, starting after 3.f3. Black's replies in the Explorer: 3...dxe4 in 50% of the games, 3...e6 in 30%, 3...g6 in 20% (made-up numbers, as all numbers below).

From the ends of the lines back to the start:

  • 3...dxe4 4.fxe4: your preparation stops with 4.fxe4, so this line is worth 4.fxe4's own results in the position after 3...dxe4: 58%.
  • 3...e6 4.Nc3: Black's replies are weighted again. 4...Bb4 is answered by 5.a3, where your preparation stops: 55%, 5.a3's own results. 4...dxe4 isn't answered, so its own results count: 48%. This branch is worth 60% × 55% + 40% × 48% = 52.2%.
  • 3...g6 isn't answered: its own results, 51%.

Expected score = 50% × 58% + 30% × 52.2% + 20% × 51% = 29 + 15.66 + 10.2 = 54.86%

Where a line ends

A line ends with its last answered move. Usually that's one of your moves, after which you haven't prepared anything. An opponent move in your study that you haven't prepared an answer to isn't an answered move, and is ignored, as if it weren't in the study.

Say a line ends with 5.b3 e4, and you're White: 5...e4 has no prepared answer, so the line ends with 5.b3. Its value is 5.b3's own results: the games in which 5.b3 was played in the position before it. The Explorer shows them on 5.b3's row when you look at the position after Black's 4th move. It isn't an average over Black's replies after 5.b3.

Every case, in one table:

WhereWhat counts
The line ends with your move (no opponent reply after it is answered)That move's own results, as listed in the position before it.
An opponent reply that isn't in your studyThat reply's own results; the line ends there.
An opponent reply in your study, without a prepared answerIgnored: the same as a reply that isn't in your study.
A move that ends the game (mate, stalemate, bare kings)The exact result: a win 1, a draw ½, a loss 0. It needs no answer.
Your last move is too rare for the Explorer to list itThe results of the replies to it, weighted as usual.
A position with no games in the Explorer½, a neutral score.
The starting point itself, with nothing preparedThe starting position's own results.

Why the move's own results

The Explorer works by position, not by move order. The list of replies in a position counts every game that reached it, by any move order, and then played that reply. So when a line's value is the results of its last move, the weights and the values come from the same games, and two useful properties follow:

  • Preparing nothing changes nothing. Without any preparation, a position's expected score is exactly its own score in the Explorer: the weighted average of its replies' results is the position's result.
  • Only your preparation moves the score. Writing down a move of your opponent's without preparing an answer doesn't change the expected score.

The position after your last move, by contrast, can also be reached by other move orders, played by other players with other plans. Using its results would mix in games that never went through your line, and the score could change without any change in your preparation.

Coverage

Coverage is the share of games still in your preparation after each of your opponent's moves, with the same weights. It starts at 100%. Your moves keep the games in; each opponent move splits them by how often each reply is played; games on a reply your study doesn't answer leave your preparation. So coverage can only go down, and only on your opponent's moves.

What the expected score does and doesn't say

  • It overstates your score where your preparation ends. Explorer results include how the players continued after your last move, sometimes with preparation you may not have. This isn't a flaw in using real games; it's a limit of treating the pool's average results as a stand-in for your own play once you're out of book.
  • Rare replies are left out. The Explorer lists the most played moves in a position (up to 12); the weights are shares of those listed moves.
  • It depends on the pool. The rating band and time controls in the study's calculation settings choose the games; results calculated with different settings aren't comparable, which is why the leaderboards rank them separately.
  • Small samples are noisy. A move's results over a few dozen games are uncertain by several points; the game counts are shown in the Explorer.

The expected evaluation walks the same tree with the same weights, but values the end of each line with Stockfish's evaluation: of the position after each of your opponent's likely replies (weighted by how often they're played) when the line ends with your move, and of the position itself after a reply your study doesn't answer. See the Stats documentation for both calculations' details.
